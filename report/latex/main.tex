\begin{abstract}
Deep architectures have recently been proposed for cardiovascular disease classification from the CDC Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS), but reported metrics can be difficult to interpret when resampling, prevalence, and threshold selection are incompletely specified. We perform a leakage-aware structural reimplementation and extension of EnsCVDD-Net on the 253,680-record BRFSS 2015 Heart Disease Health Indicators cohort. Raw rows are split before learned preprocessing; validation and test partitions retain natural prevalence; and all reported operating thresholds and calibrators are learned without access to test labels. The published combination of accuracy, precision, and recall is mathematically incompatible with the cohort prevalence: the reported precision and recall imply approximately 52.3\% positive prevalence and approximately 98.5\% accuracy under a simple confusion-matrix reconstruction, but this does not identify the original split or resampling procedure. Across five persisted repeated holdouts, Histogram Gradient Boosting has the highest mean PR-AUC ($0.3717\pm0.0067$) and ROC-AUC ($0.8483\pm0.0026$). Domain-grouped neural models exceed the sequential CNN--GRU reimplementation in PR-AUC, whereas learned group gating does not outperform uniform pooling. An 80/20 HistGradientBoosting--FGGN probability blend is numerically strongest ($0.3728\pm0.0064$ PR-AUC), but the improvement is small and not independently established as statistically significant. Validation-only isotonic calibration reduces the mean test Brier score from $0.1654$ to $0.0703$, below the constant-prevalence baseline of $0.0853$. Subgroup and decision-curve analyses show why natural prevalence and operating policy must accompany discrimination metrics. These findings support reproducible evaluation of self-reported heart-disease history classification; they do not establish prospective cardiovascular risk prediction or clinical readiness.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cardiovascular disease (CVD) is a major public-health burden and a frequent target for population risk stratification. Large health surveys can support inexpensive first-stage screening because they capture information about diagnosed conditions, health status, behavior, healthcare access, and demographics at scale. The Behavioral Risk Factor Surveillance System (BRFSS) is particularly useful for methodological research because its variables are widely available, low cost, and mostly categorical or ordinal.

This setting is not equivalent to prospective prediction of a future cardiovascular event. The target used here, \texttt{HeartDiseaseorAttack}, records a respondent's self-reported history of coronary heart disease or myocardial infarction at the survey time. We therefore study classification of an existing self-reported history, not diagnosis, time-to-event prediction, or prediction of future disease. This distinction matters for interpretation, transportability, and any eventual clinical validation.

The cohort is also strongly imbalanced: the positive prevalence is $9.42\%$. A classifier that always predicts the negative class obtains $90.58\%$ accuracy and zero sensitivity. Accuracy alone therefore rewards the majority class and does not describe screening value. Precision-recall area under the curve (PR-AUC), sensitivity, specificity, precision, calibration, confusion counts, and decision utility are required to understand performance in this regime. For a screening policy, sensitivity is often constrained first and a threshold is then selected to reduce unnecessary referrals. The threshold itself is a decision learned from validation data, not a property of the ranking model.

Khan \emph{et al.} proposed EnsCVDD-Net, a hybrid one-dimensional convolutional and gated recurrent architecture, and BlCVDD-Net, a blending model, for this BRFSS-derived task~\cite{khan2024heart}. Their reported accuracy, precision, and recall motivate an audit because a metric triplet can imply a different evaluation prevalence from the natural cohort. The architecture also treats the feature columns as a sequence, although the order of survey questions is not a physical or temporal coordinate. These concerns motivate a controlled reimplementation and a domain-grouped extension.

\subsection{Research Questions}
The study addresses four questions:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{RQ1: Metric consistency and leakage.} Are the published claims reproducible when raw rows are split before preprocessing and evaluation prevalence remains natural?
    \item \textbf{RQ2: Inductive bias.} Does grouping predictors by survey and health domain improve over an arbitrary sequential CNN--GRU representation?
    \item \textbf{RQ3: Gating value.} Does learned group weighting improve ranking over an otherwise identical uniform-pooling model?
    \item \textbf{RQ4: Operational evaluation.} How do calibration, recall-constrained thresholds, decision curves, and subgroup prevalence affect interpretation?
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Contributions}
Our contributions are: (i) an auditable structural reimplementation of the paper-family model under natural-prevalence evaluation; (ii) an explicit prevalence-consistency calculation that avoids inferring the original split procedure; (iii) FGGN and UniformGroupNet, which isolate domain grouping from learned gating; (iv) five-seed persistence and reconciliation of 951,300 test predictions; (v) validation-only probability calibration, subgroup, deduplication, resampling, explainability, and decision-curve analyses; and (vi) a critical account of what the existing artifacts do and do not support. The models are structural reimplementations because executable source code for the original paper was not supplied.

\section{Research Gaps and Contribution Map}
Prior work leaves six connected gaps. First, published metrics may be reported under a prevalence different from the natural cohort, making accuracy and precision comparisons ambiguous. Second, ADASYN and SMOTE interpolate encoded survey variables and can create fractional values for binary or ordinal responses~\cite{chawla2002smote,he2008adasyn}. Third, sequential convolutions impose locality on an unordered table. Fourth, a learned feature gate can appear interpretable without demonstrating incremental ranking value. Fifth, calibration and operational thresholds are often secondary to accuracy. Sixth, single-seed results do not reveal split sensitivity.

\section{Related Work}
\subsection{Machine Learning for Cardiovascular Disease Prediction}
Classical cardiovascular prediction models established the value of combining demographic, clinical, and behavioral predictors, with the Framingham equations providing a prominent example of multivariable risk estimation~\cite{dagsostino2008general}. Contemporary reviews find that machine learning can model nonlinear interactions and may improve discrimination in some settings, but they also emphasize heterogeneity in outcome definitions, external validation, and calibration~\cite{krittanawong2020machine,liu2019machine}. A systematic review of cardiovascular prediction models is therefore relevant to this study not because BRFSS classification is a replacement for clinical risk equations, but because it highlights the distinction between discrimination, calibration, and transportability.

Khan \emph{et al.} introduced EnsCVDD-Net and BlCVDD-Net for the BRFSS-derived heart-disease-history task~\cite{khan2024heart}. Their CNN--GRU design combines local convolutional processing, recurrent aggregation, and ensemble blending. The approach is relevant as a paper-family benchmark, but its interpretation depends on the prevalence and resampling boundary. Our work does not claim that the original authors used a particular hidden split; instead, it shows what prevalence is implied by the published metric combination and evaluates a controlled alternative.

\subsection{Deep Learning Architectures for Healthcare and Tabular Data}
Neural models are attractive in healthcare because they can learn nonlinear representations and combine heterogeneous inputs. In tabular data, however, the inductive structure differs from images and time series. TabNet uses sequential attentive masks to select features at decision steps and offers an interpretable mask mechanism~\cite{arik2021tabnet}. NODE uses oblivious differentiable trees to combine neural optimization with tree-like partitions~\cite{popov2020neural}. FT-Transformer tokenizes individual features and applies transformer layers to tabular inputs~\cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}, while SAINT adds row attention and contrastive pretraining to column attention~\cite{somepalli2021saint}. These methods demonstrate that modern tabular networks need not assume a simple sequential convolution, but they also show that additional flexibility does not guarantee improvement on medium-sized, low-cardinality tables.

Our FGGN is narrower than these architectures. It does not claim state-of-the-art tabular representation learning; it tests whether a transparent, pre-specified grouping of survey variables provides a useful inductive bias for this task. Its gate operates over four group embeddings rather than raw feature tokens, so gate weights are model allocations and not causal effects.

\subsection{Tree Ensembles versus Neural Networks}
Large empirical studies report that gradient-boosted decision trees remain difficult to beat on typical tabular datasets. Grinsztajn \emph{et al.} attribute this advantage partly to robustness to uninformative features, irregular functions, and feature rotations~\cite{grinsztajn2022tree}. Shwartz-Ziv and Armon similarly conclude that tree ensembles often outperform neural networks without extensive tuning~\cite{shwartz2022tabular}. These results motivate HistGradientBoosting as a serious baseline rather than a weak comparator. Our results are consistent with that literature: the tree model ranks first, while grouped neural models improve over the sequential paper-family implementation but do not clearly exceed the tree.

\subsection{Class Imbalance and Synthetic Oversampling}
SMOTE creates minority examples by interpolation between neighbors~\cite{chawla2002smote}; ADASYN makes the synthetic density adaptive to local difficulty~\cite{he2008adasyn}. Both can be useful for continuous data, but interpolation is not automatically meaningful for binary flags, ordinal scales, or survey codes. Sampling must also occur inside training folds: resampling before a split allows near-duplicates or synthetic descendants to influence evaluation. Class weighting changes the loss without creating new records, although it can distort probability estimates. Wallace and Dahabreh discuss this probability-estimation problem in imbalanced settings~\cite{wallace2012cost}. We therefore treat ADASYN as an ablation and preserve natural validation and test prevalence.

\subsection{Feature Selection, Attention, and Feature-Group Learning}
Point-biserial screening, permutation importance, and tree-based importance are useful descriptive tools but can be unstable under correlated predictors. Attention and gating mechanisms offer a learned alternative, as illustrated by TabNet~\cite{arik2021tabnet} and variable-selection components in temporal fusion transformers~\cite{lim2021temporal}. Nevertheless, an attention value is not a causal effect and an expressive gate can fail to improve generalization. FGGN tests this issue directly by comparing learned softmax pooling with equal pooling over the same semantic groups. The resulting neutral comparison is important: grouping and gating should not be conflated.

\subsection{Calibration and Clinical Prediction Evaluation}
A ranking model and a probability model are different objects. Van Calster \emph{et al.} describe a hierarchy from calibration-in-the-large and calibration slope to more demanding forms of calibration~\cite{vancalster2016calibration}. Platt scaling and isotonic regression are common post-hoc approaches~\cite{platt1999probabilistic,niculescu2005predicting}, while modern neural networks can be overconfident even when discrimination is acceptable~\cite{guo2017calibration}. We fit calibrators on validation predictions only and evaluate on untouched test predictions. PR-AUC is emphasized under imbalance because precision depends on prevalence; the relationship between ROC and PR displays is discussed by Saito and Rehmsmeier~\cite{saito2015precision}.

Decision curve analysis translates a probability threshold into standardized net benefit and compares a model with screen-all and screen-none policies~\cite{vickers2006decision}. It is useful here only as a decision-theoretic sensitivity analysis: the BRFSS target is not a validated clinical endpoint and the threshold probability is not a guideline recommendation.

\subsection{Statistical Evaluation and Reproducibility}
Repeated holdout results are dependent because training sets overlap. Nadeau and Bengio proposed a corrected resampled variance estimator for this setting~\cite{nadeau2003inference}; Dietterich discussed the hazards of naive tests for classifier comparisons~\cite{dietterich1998approximate}. The correction is used here as a sensitivity analysis for five repeated holdouts, not as a guarantee that five splits establish population-level superiority. Bootstrap intervals and Wilcoxon signed-rank results are reported alongside corrected intervals. Reproducibility also requires explicit data contracts, versioned artifacts, untouched evaluation sets, and a clear separation between exploratory and confirmatory analyses. Reporting guidance such as TRIPOD and PROBAST further emphasizes outcome definition, validation, calibration, and risk of bias~\cite{collins2015tripod,wolff2019probast}.

\subsection{Integrity, Duplicates, and Prevalence}
The audit checks binary target values, duplicate column names, numeric encoding, missingness, duplicate rows, ranges, and the dataset hash. It identifies 23,899 duplicate rows. In a low-cardinality survey, identical predictor vectors can represent different respondents rather than clerical copies. The primary analysis therefore retains all rows for comparability, while a persisted deduplication sensitivity audit removes repeated rows and compares model rankings. Removing duplicates changes prevalence to $10.32\%$ and changes HistGradientBoosting PR-AUC from $0.3732$ to $0.3835$ in that sensitivity run; the ranking order remains unchanged.

The current artifacts do not contain a duplicate-group-aware split in which every identical predictor vector is assigned to only one partition. Consequently, the primary split can contain repeated feature vectors across train, validation, and test. This is a material limitation and is not described as resolved by the row-level deduplication sensitivity run.

\subsection{Partitioning and Leakage Boundary}
For each seed, raw rows are stratified into 70\% training ($N=177{,}576$), 15\% validation ($N=38{,}052$), and 15\% test ($N=38{,}052$). The positive count in each test partition is 3,584 and natural prevalence is retained. Scaling, PBCC ranking, class-weight calculation, model fitting, threshold selection, and calibration fitting are bounded by the appropriate training or validation data. No test labels are used for model selection, threshold selection, or calibration. ADASYN is used only in training-side ablations.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Prevalence Audit of Published Metrics}
Let $\pi$ denote positive prevalence, $r$ recall, and $p$ precision. If a reported triplet is internally consistent, the implied prevalence is
\begin{equation}
\pi = \frac{r\pi}{p} + (1-r)\pi + \frac{r\pi(1-p)}{p}.
\end{equation}
Equivalently, setting $TP=r\pi N$, $FN=(1-r)\pi N$, and $FP=TP(1-p)/p$, accuracy is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Acc}=1-\pi+\pi r-\frac{\pi r(1-p)}{p}.
\end{equation}
Substituting $p=0.96$, $r=0.878$, and the natural $\pi=0.0942$ yields approximately $98.5\%$ accuracy, not $91.7\%$. Solving for prevalence using the reported accuracy instead gives an implied prevalence near $52.3\%$. This establishes an arithmetic inconsistency and an implied evaluation prevalence; it does not prove whether the original authors used pre-split oversampling, a different test subset, rounding, or another procedure.

\subsection{Feature-Group Gated Network}
Let $\mathbf{x}_g\in\mathbb{R}^{d_g}$ be the variables in group $g$, with four groups defined in Table~\ref{tab:predictors}. Each group has an MLP encoder $E_g$ producing $\mathbf{z}_g=E_g(\mathbf{x}_g)\in\mathbb{R}^{16}$. FGGN concatenates the embeddings and computes
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}=\mathrm{softmax}(W_{\mathrm{gate}}[\mathbf{z}_1;\ldots;\mathbf{z}_4]+\mathbf{b}_{\mathrm{gate}}),
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{w}$ lies on the four-dimensional probability simplex. The fused representation is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}=\sum_{g=1}^{4}w_g\mathbf{z}_g,
\qquad \hat{p}=\sigma(C(\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}})).
\end{equation}
The gate is a predictive routing device; its weights must not be interpreted as causal importance.

\subsection{UniformGroupNet and InstanceGatedNet}
UniformGroupNet uses the same group encoders but replaces learned weights with $\mathbf{z}_{\mathrm{fused}}=\frac{1}{4}\sum_g\mathbf{z}_g$. This isolates the incremental value of gating. InstanceGatedNet adds a nonlinear bottleneck before the softmax, allowing patient-specific routing. It is treated as an extension and its result is not used to rewrite the primary FGGN comparison.

\subsection{Paper-Family and Tabular Baselines}
EnsCVDD-Net is reimplemented as a sequential 1-D CNN--GRU model over the prescribed column order. This reproduces the stated structure, not necessarily every undocumented training detail of the original. BlCVDD-Net is represented in the existing ensemble artifacts where available. HistGradientBoosting is the principal non-neural baseline; logistic regression, random forests, Extra Trees, and a majority classifier provide additional reference points. The tree baseline is important because tabular empirical studies find that boosted trees often handle irregular interactions and irrelevant features better than generic neural networks~\cite{grinsztajn2022tree}.

\subsection{Optimization, Loss, and Thresholds}
Neural models use binary cross-entropy with class weighting computed from the training partition. The primary multi-seed neural comparison uses class weighting rather than global oversampling. ADASYN and no-resampling results are retained as ablations. The validation threshold is selected as
\begin{equation}
\tau^\star=\max\{\tau\in[0,1]:\mathrm{Recall}_{\mathrm{val}}(\tau)\geq0.80\}.
\end{equation}
The selected threshold is frozen before test evaluation. Thus, the 80\% constraint applies to validation recall; test recall is a separately measured outcome and may be below or above 80\%. The current persisted results do not include a complete 70/80/85/90/95\% threshold grid, so no such curve is claimed here.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistical Analysis}
We report PR-AUC (average precision), ROC-AUC, recall, precision, specificity, F1, balanced accuracy, accuracy, Brier score, confusion counts, fit time, and prevalence. Brier score is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BS}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}(\hat{p}_i-y_i)^2.
\end{equation}
For five repeated holdouts, paired differences are summarized with standard intervals, bootstrap intervals, Wilcoxon signed-rank tests, and the Nadeau--Bengio corrected variance
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{Var}}_{\mathrm{corr}}=
\left(\frac{1}{K}+\frac{n_{\mathrm{test}}}{n_{\mathrm{train}}}\right)S^2.
\end{equation}
Here $K=5$, $n_{\mathrm{test}}=38{,}052$, and $n_{\mathrm{train}}=177{,}576$, giving a multiplier of approximately $0.414$. Because the five splits are not independent samples from all possible datasets, the resulting $p$-values are sensitivity analyses rather than definitive clinical evidence.

\subsection{Calibration and Decision Curves}
Platt scaling fits a logistic mapping from validation logits or scores; isotonic regression fits a monotonic validation mapping. Both are evaluated on untouched test predictions. The constant-prevalence Brier baseline is $\pi(1-\pi)=0.0853$. Decision curve net benefit is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{NB}(p_t)=\frac{TP}{N}-\frac{FP}{N}\frac{p_t}{1-p_t},
\end{equation}
where $p_t$ is a decision threshold probability. It compares with $\mathrm{NB}_{\mathrm{none}}=0$ and $\mathrm{NB}_{\mathrm{all}}=\pi-(1-\pi)p_t/(1-p_t)$. This is a decision-theoretic quantity, not a claim that a survey probability is a validated clinical risk.

\subsection{Multi-Seed Stability and Statistical Comparisons}
The 951,300 persisted predictions were recomputed with zero discrepancy against the summary metrics. FGGN versus EnsCVDD-Net has mean paired PR-AUC difference $+0.012623$ and corrected $p=0.0011$, while FGGN versus UniformGroupNet has difference $-0.000411$ and corrected $p=0.448$. The latter is evidence against an observed gating advantage, not proof that all future samples or architectures are equivalent. The Wilcoxon result for FGGN versus EnsCVDD-Net is $p=0.0625$, illustrating sensitivity to the small number of paired seeds and why significance claims are reported with the test choice and correction.

\subsection{Precision--Recall and ROC Analysis}
PR-AUC is the primary ranking metric because the positive class is uncommon and precision changes with prevalence. Figure~\ref{fig:pr} summarizes the existing PR-AUC comparison; the gap between the majority baseline and the learned models is more informative than accuracy alone. ROC-AUC remains useful as a prevalence-insensitive ranking measure, but it can look strong while positive predictive value remains modest. The natural-prevalence precision values near $24$--$25\%$ at the recall-constrained operating point quantify the referral burden that an accuracy summary would hide.

\subsection{Recall-Constrained Threshold Evaluation}
The validation-selected threshold targets at least 80\% validation recall. On the untouched tests, mean recall ranges from $79.60\%$ to $79.82\%$ across the main models. This slight shortfall is expected under sampling variability and confirms why it is incorrect to describe the test result as a guaranteed 80\% constraint. The associated precision is approximately $24\%$--$25\%$ and specificity approximately $74\%$--$75\%. A full sensitivity curve at 70, 80, 85, 90, and 95\% recall requires a new threshold-grid export and is not inserted without evidence.

\subsection{Calibration Comparison}
Class weighting preserves ranking but shifts the score distribution, producing an uncalibrated mean test Brier score of $0.1654$ for HistGradientBoosting, worse than the constant-prevalence baseline $0.0853$. Validation-only Platt scaling reduces it to $0.0706 and isotonic regression to $0.0703. The isotonic calibration slope and intercept are approximately $0.963$ and $-0.058$, respectively. \subsection{Hybrid Ensemble}
An 80/20 probability blend of HistGradientBoosting and FGGN reaches PR-AUC $0.3728\pm0.0064$ and ROC-AUC $0.8489\pm0.0025$. The numerical gain suggests complementary errors, but the improvement is small. Because the blend weight and model selection are part of the experimental process, the result should be treated as an extension requiring further nested validation or external confirmation rather than as proof of superiority.

\subsection{Subgroup Analysis}
The subgroup artifacts evaluate age, sex, and income strata. Age prevalence is $1.38\%$ for young adults, $7.08\%$ for middle-aged adults, and $17.25\%$ for older adults; sex prevalence is $7.24\%$ for females and $12.18\%$ for males; income prevalence ranges from $5.97\%$ in the highest tier to $15.96\%$ in the lowest. Precision is consequently prevalence-sensitive: the same ranking and threshold can yield much lower positive predictive value in a low-prevalence group. These results are not a fairness certification, and the available analysis does not identify causal sources of subgroup differences.

\section{Discussion}
HistGradientBoosting remains competitive because the predictors are low-cardinality, mixed-scale, and tabular, with nonlinear interactions and no meaningful spatial adjacency. This agrees with broad tabular benchmarks~\cite{grinsztajn2022tree,shwartz2022tabular}. Domain grouping helps neural models by replacing arbitrary adjacency with a modest prior over related variables and by giving each group its own encoder. The result does not show that the grouping is universally optimal; it shows that this specified grouping is a useful controlled alternative in this cohort.

The neutral FGGN--UniformGroupNet result is substantively important. Learned gating does not automatically produce a predictive gain, and gate weights should not be presented as evidence that one domain is causally more important. The hybrid blend is slightly stronger than either component, suggesting complementary errors, but its small margin and selection process warrant conservative interpretation.

Class weighting improves attention to the minority class while shifting probabilities away from the natural posterior. The calibration results show why discrimination and calibration must be reported separately. Natural-prevalence evaluation also explains the modest precision at a high-recall operating point: even a useful ranking model faces a low base rate. Subgroup prevalence differences can change precision without a corresponding change in ranking quality, so one global threshold can produce different referral burdens across populations.

The paper-family arithmetic audit should be interpreted narrowly. The published metric combination is incompatible with the natural prevalence under a simple confusion-matrix calculation and implies a substantially more balanced evaluation distribution. It does not reveal the original authors' exact procedure or establish misconduct. Similarly, the Nadeau--Bengio correction makes the dependence assumption explicit but cannot compensate for having only five repeated holdouts.

\section{Limitations and Future Work}
The outcome is self-reported, cross-sectional, and limited to the 2015 BRFSS-derived file. It is not a future-event endpoint and does not include laboratory values, clinical examination, longitudinal follow-up, or external validation. The dataset is old relative to current populations, and survey coding and missingness mechanisms may change over time. Feature-group definitions are subjective. Five repeated holdouts provide useful stability evidence but are not a substitute for nested resampling or prospective validation. Duplicate feature vectors may cross partitions in the primary row-level split; duplicate-group-aware evaluation remains necessary. Calibration under distribution shift has not been established, and the decision curves depend on unvalidated costs and thresholds.

Future work should include duplicate-group-aware splits, a preregistered recall-target threshold grid, external validation on newer survey years and clinical cohorts, temporal and geographic robustness, subgroup calibration and fairness analysis, sensitivity to alternative encodings, stronger tabular architectures, and prospective clinical workflow validation. Any claimed improvement should be measured on the same untouched test design and accompanied by absolute metrics, uncertainty, prevalence, and calibration.

\section{Conclusion}
This study turns a high-accuracy paper-family claim into a reproducible natural-prevalence evaluation of self-reported heart-disease history classification. The results favor HistGradientBoosting for ranking, show that semantic grouping improves over the sequential CNN--GRU reimplementation, and provide no evidence that learned group gating improves over uniform pooling. Validation-only isotonic calibration materially improves probability quality, while subgroup and decision-curve analyses demonstrate that screening interpretation depends on prevalence and operating policy. The work is a methodological research prototype, not a clinical tool or a prospective cardiovascular risk model.
